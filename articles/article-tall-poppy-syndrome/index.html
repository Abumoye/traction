Tall Poppy Syndrome: Why Your Closest Friends Are Often the Last to Become Your Customers

Traction Outsourcing Limited July 14, 2026 4 min read
Back to Articles

Your friends know exactly what you do. They came to the launch, liked the posts, then bought the exact same thing from someone else. If that has happened to you, it is rarely as personal as it feels. It has a name, and it is older than you think.

An Old Instinct With a New Name

Tall Poppy Syndrome was named in Australia and New Zealand in the 1980s, but the instinct predates the phrase by two thousand years. The Roman historian Livy recorded the source of the name: King Tarquinius Superbus, asked by his son how to control a rival city, said nothing. He simply walked into his garden and knocked the heads off the tallest poppies. Japan has its own version, the nail that sticks up gets hammered down. Chile calls it chaquetear, to pull someone back by the jacket. People rarely resent your growth because they dislike you. They resent it because it measures the distance between where you have gone and where you both expected to stay.

What the Research Shows

In 2018, Women of Influence surveyed 1,501 people across Canadian workplaces. Nearly nine in ten said their achievements had been undermined at work, and about half named their own friends as the source. A 2023 follow-up surveyed 4,710 women across 103 countries and found 86.8 percent had experienced the same pattern. Undermining someone privately and simply choosing not to pay them for their work are the same instinct, expressed differently.

Choosing not to buy from a friend's business rarely feels like jealousy from the inside. It feels like loyalty to an old version of the relationship, one where nobody had to pay anybody for anything.

Why Friends Pull Back

Most friendships form between people close in circumstance and stage of life. That closeness is the floor the friendship stands on. When one person starts visibly rising, the floor tilts. Access that used to be free now has a price attached, and paying you can feel like renegotiating a friendship rather than simply buying a service. Nobody frames it as jealousy to themselves, but not buying quietly protects the old equality all the same.

Grace, Not Grudges

Not every friend who never became a customer is cutting you down. Some cannot afford what you offer, some do not need it, others are loyal to a provider they already trust. What is worth naming is different: the friend who dismisses your work to others or spends money elsewhere just to make a point. That is Tall Poppy Syndrome in its sharper form.

What This Means for Your Business

None of this means shrinking to stay comfortable for people. It means being realistic about where your early revenue comes from. Friends who knew you before you built anything are often the last to buy, because buying from you means mentally reclassifying you from friend to service provider. Your first real customers are more likely to be strangers who meet you as a business owner from day one, with no old relationship to renegotiate.

That is where structure earns its keep. A stranger has no history with you to fall back on, so what convinces them is what they can see: clear process, a professional handbook, a team that knows its roles. Startup structuring builds exactly that foundation. Business advisory support helps founders further along see the gaps a friend's goodwill used to paper over, and staff outsourcing puts the right people in place to carry it. Frequently Asked Questions

What is Tall Poppy Syndrome in business?
Tall Poppy Syndrome is the social instinct to resent or pull back from someone who visibly outgrows their peer group. In business, it often shows up as friends and early acquaintances becoming quieter supporters, not louder ones, once a founder's business starts to succeed.
Why do friends struggle to support a growing business?
Friendship is usually built on relative equality. When one person visibly grows through a business, a promotion, or new opportunities, that balance shifts. It is rarely malice. More often it is discomfort with a relationship that no longer feels level, and that discomfort quietly shows up as friends going elsewhere for what you sell.
Should a founder stop growing to avoid Tall Poppy Syndrome?
No. The answer is not to shrink to keep people comfortable. It is to understand where your early customers will realistically come from, usually people who meet you as a business owner first, and to build the structure that lets strangers trust you quickly since your oldest friends may take longer.
