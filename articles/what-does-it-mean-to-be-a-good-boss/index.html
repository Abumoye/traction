What Does It Mean to Be a Good Boss?

Traction Outsourcing Limited November 3, 2026 6 min read
Back to Articles

During one of our staff trainings at Traction Outsourcing, a simple question took over the room for thirty minutes: what makes a boss good? We expected a few quick answers before moving on to the next session. Instead, the room split into camps.

One participant said a good boss must be a friend to the people who work for them. A friend listens, understands when your child is sick, and does not make you afraid to knock on the office door. Several heads nodded. Then someone at the back disagreed strongly. In their view, a boss who becomes too friendly loses respect, and staff start treating deadlines as suggestions. A good boss, they argued, should keep some distance, even appear cold, so that instructions are taken seriously. Others took positions somewhere in between. When the facilitator finally called time, nobody had changed their mind.

That debate plays out in offices across Nigeria and family businesses across the continent. It never ends because most people judge a boss by one quality, usually the one they missed most in their last job.

This article has two aims. The first is to help employees recognise a good boss when they find one. The second is to help employers and managers see what they must do to become better bosses. From our work with businesses across Nigeria and Africa, a good boss carries six roles at once: a Leader, a Mentor, an Executionist, a Warden, a Conscience and a Builder. Few bosses are strong in all six. The good ones know where they are weak and work on it.

A Leader: Someone Who Shows the Way

Leadership is the ability to set a direction and get people to follow it willingly. A title gives a boss authority, but authority alone only gets people to comply. Leadership gives them a reason to follow beyond the salary.

As an employee, you will recognise a leader as the boss who can explain where the business is going and why your work matters to it. When the month is tough, they speak to the team plainly instead of disappearing behind a closed door. You know what is expected of you, and you trust that the person in front knows where they are going.

For employers and managers, this starts with saying the vision out loud, often and in simple words. Many owners carry the plan in their heads and assume everyone else knows it. If you want to improve your skills in leadership as a boss, share the targets for the year, explain the reasons behind big decisions, and go first when sacrifice is needed. A team will accept cost cuts more readily when it sees the boss cutting too and putting in the work.

A Mentor: Someone Who Grows People

A mentor is an experienced person who guides a less experienced one by sharing knowledge, correcting mistakes and opening doors. This is very common in Africa, where older people are expected to nurture and mentor younger ones. For generations, apprentices learnt a trade beside a master tailor or trader until the day the master settled them to start on their own.

A boss who mentors corrects you in private and explains the reason behind the correction. They give you work that is slightly harder than what you can comfortably do, then stand close enough to help. When you do well, they say so in front of others.

For employers and managers, mentoring means setting aside time for your people, even when you are busy. Hand over tasks that stretch your staff, and resist the fear that teaching someone will make you replaceable. A boss whose juniors cannot do anything without them has built a weakness into the business, and it will show the first time that boss falls sick or travels.

An Executionist: Someone Who Gets Things Done

We use the word executionist for a boss who turns plans into results. Execution means carrying a plan through to completion. This is true because, while every business has ideas, the ones that grow are run by people who follow through.

Under an executionist, you will notice clear deadlines, decisions made on time and steady follow-up. When something blocks your work, such as a missing approval, a broken printer or a supplier who will not respond, this boss removes the obstacle quickly. You do not wait three weeks for a signature.

For employers and managers, execution begins with breaking each goal into specific tasks, each with an owner and a date. Hold a short weekly review to check progress. Make decisions promptly, because a delayed decision costs money every day it waits.

A Warden: Someone Who Keeps Order

A warden is the person charged with keeping order and enforcing rules, much like the warden of a school hostel. This is where the participants in our staff training who argued for distance had a point. Discipline is part of a boss's job, and a workplace without it soon becomes unfair to the people who play by the rules.

A good warden applies the same rules to everyone, including the favourite manager or supervisor and the owner's relative. Consequences are predictable, so nobody has to guess how a mistake will be treated. Employees under this boss feel protected, because bullies, cheats and chronic latecomers are checked before they spoil the place.

For employers, put your rules in an employee handbook so staff know them before they break them. Correct problems early, calmly and privately, and keep a record. Firmness does not require shouting. The best wardens are respected because they are fair. If you need a company handbook, reach out to our team at Traction Outsourcing.

A Conscience: Someone Who Does What Is Right

A conscience is the inner sense of right and wrong. A boss serves as the conscience of a business when their conduct sets its moral standard. Staff watch closely, and they copy what the boss does far more than what the boss says.

As an employee, you will see this in a boss who pays salaries when due, or explains honestly when there is a delay. They do not ask you to falsify a record or lie to a client. They keep their promises, admit their own mistakes and treat you with dignity, even when they are correcting you.

It is important for employers and managers to remember that every small act teaches. If you ask the receptionist to tell a visitor you are out while you sit in your office, you have taught her that lying is acceptable. Teach your staff and team members to be honourable by being honourable, and where necessary, acknowledge your own faults.

A Builder: Someone Who Leaves Something Behind

A builder creates structures, systems and people that will outlast them. Too many African businesses close soon after their founders step back, because everything depended on one person.

A builder boss writes down how work is done, creates clear career paths and invests in training. Employees under this kind of boss can see a future in the company, because they can see how people grow within it.

For employers, ask yourself a hard question: if you left for a month, what would break? Whatever comes to mind is where building should begin. Draw an organogram, document your key processes, plan who could step into each senior role, and set aside a budget for training every year.
