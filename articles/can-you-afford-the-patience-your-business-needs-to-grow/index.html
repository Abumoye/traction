Can You Afford The Patience Your Business Needs To Grow?

Traction Outsourcing Limited September 30, 2026 6 min read
Back to Articles

Every year, thousands of Nigerians resign from paid jobs, gather their savings and open a restaurant in Wuse, a boutique in Lekki or a logistics company in Onitsha. Most of them expect the business to pay its own bills within six months and give them a comfortable stream of income within one year. When that does not happen, many close shop, sell the equipment at a loss and decide that the business is not for them.

For many of these young, vibrant Nigerians, the idea was sound. What ran out first was TIME.

A business needs time to find its customers, fix its prices, learn what sells and survive its first bad season. That period of waiting, learning and adjusting is what we mean by patience in business. Most people do not know that patience has a price. Someone has to pay the rent, the staff and the founder's own living costs while the business finds its feet. Founders who cannot pay it are often forced to quit before the business has had a fair chance.

The Data Is the Proof

A survival rate is the share of new businesses still operating after a given number of years. Across the world, survival rates for new businesses are low, and the African figures are harsh.

Put simply, if ten people in Lagos opened businesses this year, the data suggests only about two would still be operating five years from now. More often than not, those two are the ones who could afford to wait.

Why Some Founders Can Wait and Others Cannot

Think of patience as a cushion. This cushion is the set of resources outside the business that keeps a founder standing while the business is still weak. We have listed the most common cushions below.

Cushion 1: A Family That Can Absorb Failure

A founder from a rich or middle-class home can fail and try again. If the first business closes, they move back into the family house or get a family apartment. A parent can offer to fund the second attempt, and family friends become the first customers. Research published by the NBER, based in the United States, found that people who run incorporated businesses tend to come from higher-income families. A founder without this cushion often gets only one attempt, and first attempts are where the most costly lessons are learned.

Cushion 2: A Spouse Who Earns and Believes

When a husband or wife has a steady source of income, essential household costs like school fees, food and rent are covered. The founder can go without a salary for a year or more and put every naira of profit back into the business.

The support runs deeper than money. A spouse who says, "I want you to try again," after a bad quarter gives the founder room to think clearly. A supportive spouse brings calm in the middle of a turbulent storm.

Cushion 3: A Salary That Still Comes In

Keeping a job while building a business is often mocked as a lack of commitment, yet a study in the Academy of Management Journal found that founders who kept their day jobs while starting their businesses were about a third less likely to fail than those who quit to go full time. A salary buys time to test the idea, find paying customers and make mistakes cheaply.

Cushion 4: Access to Credit When Cash Runs Short

Credit here means more than a bank loan. It includes a supplier who lets you take goods today and pay in thirty days, a cooperative society, an ajo or esusu contribution group, or a relative who lends without interest. The founder who can borrow cheaply survives a bad month. The founder whose only option is a high-interest loan app often sinks deeper with every repayment.

Cushion 5: Emotional Safety

In many of our communities, failure is public. People discuss it at family meetings, in church and in WhatsApp groups. A founder who fears that shame will either quit early or hide problems until they are too big to fix. Research shows that a founder who feels safe can admit a mistake early, change direction and keep going. This cushion functions as an emotional safety net, keeping founders steady while they handle the many moving parts of a business. That safety can come from a spouse, a mentor, or a community of fellow business owners.

The Silent Killer: When the Business Becomes the Family Purse

One of the most common and least discussed reasons small businesses die in Africa is the founder using company money to meet personal and family obligations. School fees are due in September. A parent needs a hospital bill paid. There is a burial in the village, a wedding with its aso ebi, a sibling's rent. The founder has no personal cushion, so the money comes from the only place it can: the business account. This is called Black Tax.

When Black Tax enters a business, it takes away the ability to run the business efficiently and reinvest in what matters most.

A 2024 study of Kenyan business owners found that about 1 in 3 founders faced strong pressure to share income with relatives, and those under pressure were less likely to invest extra cash in their businesses. Another study of Ghanaian businesses listed diversion of business funds among the most frequent causes of failure, with loans meant for operations spent on private cars and private buildings.

How to Buy Patience You Were Not Born With

If you did not inherit any of the five cushions above, you can still build some of them before and during the life of your business.

  • Separate the money. Open a business account and never pay personal bills from it.
  • Pay yourself a fixed salary, however small, and live on it.
  • Build a personal runway of six to twelve months of living costs before leaving paid work.
  • Keep your job or another income for as long as you can while you test the idea.
  • Start small. Rent less, hire slowly and share space where possible.
  • Talk to your family early about what the business can and cannot carry.
  • Build credit relationships before you need them, with suppliers, cooperatives and your bank.
  • Plan for years, not months. Budget as if profit will take three years to arrive.

What You Must Never Forget

Business rewards the people who stay long enough to learn. It takes time, effort, finance and mental agility to run and build a successful business. Before you resign, sign a lease or take a loan, ask yourself if you can afford the patience the business requires.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do most small businesses in Nigeria fail within five years?
Data from the Kippa MSME Report shows about 80% of small businesses in Nigeria fail within five years. The most common reason is not a bad idea but running out of time: founders often cannot personally afford to keep paying their bills long enough for the business to become profitable.
What is a 'cushion' in the context of starting a business?
A cushion is a resource outside the business that keeps a founder financially and emotionally stable while the business is still weak. Common cushions include family wealth, a working spouse, a salary from another job, access to affordable credit and emotional support from family or a community.
What is Black Tax and how does it affect a business?
Black Tax refers to the pressure on a founder to use business income to cover personal and family financial obligations, such as school fees, medical bills or family ceremonies. A 2024 study found that about 1 in 3 Kenyan founders faced strong pressure to share income with relatives, and founders under that pressure were less likely to reinvest in their businesses.
How can a founder build patience if they do not have family wealth to fall back on?
Founders can build their own cushion by separating personal and business finances, paying themselves a small fixed salary, saving six to twelve months of living costs before quitting a job, keeping other income for as long as possible, starting small, and building credit relationships with suppliers or cooperatives before they are needed.
