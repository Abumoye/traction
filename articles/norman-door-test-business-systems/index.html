The Norman Door Test: Applying Design Thinking to How Your Business Actually Runs

Traction Outsourcing Limited September 19, 2026 4 min read
Back to Articles

Walk into almost any office building in Abuja or Lagos and you will find at least one door that defeats people daily. It has a flat metal plate at chest height, the kind that only makes sense on a door you push, yet the door needs to be pulled. Visitors slow down, hesitate, push anyway, bounce off it, then pull sheepishly while glancing around to see who noticed. Nobody designed that door to embarrass people. It simply gave them no clue about what to do, and they filled the gap with a guess that happened to be wrong.

The design theorist Don Norman noticed this exact failure everywhere he looked, so often that doors like this now carry his name in design circles. He built an entire body of work, most famously the book "The Design of Everyday Things," around a single idea that sounds almost too simple to matter: when people fail to use something correctly, the fault rarely belongs to them. It belongs to whoever built the thing and assumed the user would figure it out. That idea, once you notice it, is impossible to unsee in how many Nigerian businesses are run.

What Don Norman Actually Argued

Don Norman's design thinking rests on a handful of principles that translate directly into business life. A signifier is a cue that tells a person what action to take, a flat plate for push, a handle for pull. Feedback confirms that an action worked, a light that turns on, a message that says received. Mapping is the natural link between a control and what it does, a switch positioned near the device it operates. A conceptual model is the mental picture a person builds of how something works before they even try it.

When any of these go missing, people do not fail because they are careless. They fail because they were never given anything to work with, and they had to invent their own logic on the spot. Don Norman's argument was that good design removes the guessing entirely. A business, at its core, is also a designed system, and it fails or succeeds by the same rules.

Nobody designs a door to embarrass the person walking through it. They just forget to tell them which way to push.

The Staff Version of the Norman Door

Picture a new front desk hire at a consulting firm in Abuja, three days into the job, facing an angry walk-in client for the first time. Nobody handed her a written procedure for this. She was told to use her initiative, so she does, escalating the complaint to the wrong department. Her manager later queries her for poor judgement, without pausing to ask whether the judgement was ever hers to have in the first place.

This is a Norman door wearing a staff badge. The business gave her no signifier, no signpost for what to do when a client walked in upset, and expected the correct action to occur to her naturally. When it did not, she carried the blame that belonged to the missing structure. Multiply this across every new hire and the pattern becomes visible: an owner growing frustrated that staff "cannot think," staff growing quietly resentful of being punished for something nobody taught them, and a business that keeps hiring for common sense instead of building the system that would make common sense unnecessary.

The fix costs far less than the damage it prevents. A one page escalation chart pinned near the front desk, a short onboarding pack that walks through the first ten situations a new hire will actually meet, a checklist for the tasks that repeat every week. None of this is complicated, and it is exactly the kind of documentation structured staff training is built to produce. It simply replaces a blank wall with a signifier, the flat plate that tells a person which way to push.

The Client Version of the Norman Door

The same failure happens on the other side of the counter. A client visits an office to discuss a service and finds no one has told them where to sit, who to ask for, or what happens first, the conversation or the paperwork. A job applicant submits a CV and is left to guess whether silence means rejection or simply a slow process. A client who has just signed an agreement is not told whether to expect an invoice first or a call to schedule a deposit, so they phone the office repeatedly just to find out what comes next.

None of these clients are being unreasonable. They are standing in front of a Norman door, one built from silence instead of steel, and they are doing what anyone does at a door with no signifier: guessing, hesitating, or walking away. A business that wants to keep a client through the full length of a transaction has to remove that guesswork at every step, a confirmation message after every stage, a printed or emailed next-steps sheet, and front-of-house staff who have been properly briefed to say exactly where a new visitor should go before they even have to ask.

Running the Norman Door Test

Before assuming a mistake was carelessness, on either side of the business, it is worth running a quick test. Can a new staff member complete this task correctly without first asking a colleague what to do? Can a client move to the next step without calling the office to confirm it? If ten different people faced this same point in the process, would most of them get it right on the first try, or would most of them hesitate the way visitors hesitate at a door with no signifier?

If the honest answer points to hesitation, the problem is not the person standing at that door. It is the door.

The Practical Takeaway

Don Norman's real gift to business owners is permission to stop blaming people for problems that structure created. Assuming that staff or clients will simply know what to do is the fastest route to job dissatisfaction, owner frustration, and quiet inefficiency, because it hands the outcome of every interaction over to guesswork and hopes for the best. The businesses that grow with less friction are usually the ones that have gone through their own processes, from the first day of onboarding to the last line of an invoice, and replaced every unmarked door with a clear signifier. Staff stop guessing. Clients stop calling to ask what happens next. Everyone, on both sides of the business, already knows which way to push.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a "Norman door" in a business context?
A Norman door is named after design theorist Don Norman, who wrote about doors so poorly designed that people cannot tell whether to push or pull them. In a business, a Norman door is any process, staff task, or client interaction that gives no clear signal for what to do next, forcing people to guess and often guess wrong.
Why does Don Norman say the user is rarely at fault?
Don Norman's core argument in "The Design of Everyday Things" is that when people fail to use something correctly, the fault usually belongs to whoever designed it and assumed the correct action would be obvious. Businesses that blame staff for "not thinking" or clients for "not reading instructions" are often missing a structural problem, not a people problem.
How do I know if my business has a Norman door problem?
Run a simple test: can a new staff member complete a given task correctly without first asking a colleague, and can a client move to the next step without calling the office to confirm it? If most people would hesitate or guess wrong, the process is missing a clear signifier, and that is a structural gap rather than a training failure alone.
What is the fastest way to fix a Norman door in a growing business?
Start with the highest-traffic points of confusion: a one-page escalation chart for front desk staff, a short onboarding pack covering the first situations a new hire will meet, and a confirmation message or next-steps sheet for clients after every stage of a transaction. None of these require major investment, only clear documentation of what already exists.
